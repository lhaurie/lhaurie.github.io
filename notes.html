Mathematical tools

This section contains all the mathematical background needed to understand the notes. The prerequisites are mostly linear algebra, basic analysis and statistic knowledge.

  • Submanifolds and differential geometry [Show PDF]

    A self-contained introduction to the geometric structures underlying classical and quantum mechanics: what it means for a space to be curved, constrained, or locally flat, how calculus extends to such spaces, and how far that calculus can be pushed before topology gets in the way.

    • Differentiability, Jacobians and diffeomorphisms (prerequisites): The correct notion of differentiability for maps between multi-dimensional spaces, the Jacobian as its matrix representation, and diffeomorphisms as the natural equivalence between smooth spaces. The inverse function theorem is stated: an invertible linear approximation implies a locally invertible map.
    • Submanifolds: graph and level set views (Lagrange, Gauss): A submanifold is a subset of an ambient space that locally looks like flat Euclidean space. Two equivalent descriptions: as the graph of a smooth function (free coordinates constraining dependent ones), or as the level set of a constraint map. The implicit function theorem makes this equivalence precise.
    • Tangent spaces and the regular value theorem (modern differential geometry): The tangent space at a point captures all possible velocity directions of curves on the submanifold. When constraints are independent (measured by the rank of the Jacobian) the tangent space is exactly the kernel of the constraint derivative. This is the regular value theorem.
    • Charts, atlases and generalized coordinates (Riemann, Lagrange): A chart assigns local coordinates to a patch of the manifold; an atlas covers it entirely. Generalized coordinates in mechanics are precisely chart coordinates, and a change of generalized coordinates is a transition map between charts. The coordinate-independence of the Euler-Lagrange equations follows directly from this structure.
    • Vector fields and flows (modern formulation): A vector field assigns a tangent vector to every point of the manifold, independently of any coordinate choice. Its integral curves are solutions to an ODE on the manifold. Newton's equations and the Euler-Lagrange equations both define vector fields whose integral curves are the physical trajectories of the system.
    • Duality, covectors and one-forms (Grassmann): A covector at a point is a linear functional on the tangent space; smoothly assembled over the manifold, covectors become one-forms, dual to vector fields. Vector components carry upper indices, covector components lower ones.
    • Riemannian metric and musical isomorphisms (Riemann): A metric $g$ equips each tangent space with an inner product, giving meaning to lengths, angles, and geodesics. It also induces the musical isomorphisms, a canonical identification between vectors and covectors without a metric, the two remain structurally distinct spaces.
    • Tensor and exterior algebra (Grassmann, Ricci-Curbastro): Tensor products combine vector spaces (or their duals) into higher-rank objects; their antisymmetric part is the exterior (wedge) algebra, generalizing signed area and volume to any dimension. Bivectors, k-forms, and the classical cross product all fall out as special cases, with the interior product acting as the natural degree-lowering counterpart to the wedge product.
    • Exterior derivative and Stokes' theorem (Cartan, Stokes): The exterior derivative d unifies gradient, curl and divergence into one metric-free operator, defined on forms of any degree and any dimension. Its deepest property is Stokes' theorem, generalizing the fundamental theorem of calculus and giving closed and exact forms their operational meaning.
    • Cartan's magic formula and de Rham cohomology (Cartan, de Rham): Cartan's formula defines the Lie derivative: the rate of change of a form dragged along a flow purely algebraically, with no reference to charts or limits, and is the key ingredient in Liouville's theorem. Pushed further, this machinery yields de Rham cohomology, which detects topological obstructions invisible to local calculus alone.
Classical mechanics
  • Newtonian mechanics through geometry, symmetries and conservation laws [Show PDF]

    A foundational chapter introducing classical mechanics of point-like objects from a modern perspective: frames are built using differential geometry, dynamics is formulated as a flow on state space, and conservation laws emerge from symmetry principles.

    • Frames and geometric structure of space (Newton → modern maths): Motion requires a precise mathematical structure: space is modeled as a manifold, with tangent spaces encoding velocities. A frame corresponds to choosing a basis of these tangent spaces at each point, together with time and an origin. Coordinate systems induce (holonomic) frames, while inertial frames are those where free motion is uniform and rectilinear.
    • Newton’s laws, momentum and work-energy (17th–18th century): Newton’s laws define dynamics through the evolution of momentum, while the concept of work links forces to energy variations. Kinetic energy naturally emerges from the work-energy theorem, clarifying the distinction between instantaneous forces, accumulated work, and state functions such as energy.
    • State space and dynamical systems (modern formulation): A mechanical system is described by its state (position and velocity), evolving as a trajectory in state space. Newton’s equations define a vector field whose integral curves are physical motions. Observables are functions on this space, and conservation corresponds to invariance along these trajectories.
    • Meaning of conservation laws (Euler → Noether): A quantity is conserved if it remains constant along all solutions of the equations of motion. Momentum, energy, and angular momentum are not always conserved: their conservation depends on specific physical conditions. This formalism clarifies the difference between state functions (observables) and path-dependent quantities like work.
    • Momentum conservation and translation symmetry (Newtonian mechanics): Momentum is conserved when the net external force vanishes. Physically, this reflects invariance under spatial translations: the laws of physics are identical everywhere, leading to conservation of momentum.
    • Energy conservation and time symmetry (18th–19th century): Kinetic energy alone is not generally conserved, but when forces derive from a time-independent potential, the sum of kinetic and potential energy (mechanical energy) is conserved. This reflects invariance under time translations: physics does not depend on when an experiment is performed.
    • Angular momentum and rotational symmetry (Euler, Lagrange): Angular momentum measures rotational motion and evolves according to torque. It is conserved when the total torque vanishes, typically for central forces. This conservation reflects rotational invariance and explains phenomena such as Kepler’s second law for planetary motion.
    • Symmetries as the origin of conservation (Noether’s insight): Conservation laws are deeply tied to symmetries: translation symmetry yields momentum conservation, time symmetry yields energy conservation, and rotational symmetry yields angular momentum conservation. This perspective unifies classical mechanics and prepares the transition to Lagrangian and modern physics.


  • Analytical mechanics I: constraints, Lagrangian mechanics and variational principles [Show PDF]

    A geometric reformulation of classical mechanics, adapted to constrained systems. The chapter introduces configuration spaces and generalized coordinates, eliminates constraint forces through D'Alembert's principle, and derives the Euler-Lagrange equations and Lagrangian formalism. It then develops generalized potentials, rheonomic constraints, the Hamiltonian, and the variational formulation through Hamilton's principle.

    • Constraints and configuration space (Lagrange, 18th century): Holonomic constraints restrict the accessible configurations to a smooth configuration manifold. This replaces the redundant ambient description by the geometry of the system itself and identifies the number of degrees of freedom.
    • Generalized coordinates and state space (Lagrange): Local coordinates given by charts on the configuration manifold provide independent generalized coordinates and velocities. The mechanical state is naturally represented by the tangent bundle. Despite their name, configuration and state space are not always vector space.
    • Virtual displacements and D'Alembert's principle (D'Alembert, 18th century): Virtual displacements are tangent variations of the configuration at fixed time. For holonomic constraints, constraint forces do no virtual work, allowing them to disappear from Newton's equations and reducing the dynamics to the configuration manifold.
    • Euler-Lagrange equations and the Lagrangian (Euler, Lagrange, 18th century): Expressing D'Alembert's principle in generalized coordinates leads to the Euler-Lagrange equations. The dynamics is encoded by the scalar Lagrangian (kinetic energy minus potential energy), reducing the original vector equations to one scalar equation per degree of freedom.
    • Generalized potentials, forces and energy (Lagrangian mechanics): Velocity dependent potential are naturally preserved by Euler-Lagrange dynamic, while non-conservative forces remain explicit. While the mechanical energy is then no longer conserved, a generalized version of it called "Hamiltonian" is conserved, as long as no explicit time dependence is considered.
    • Scleronomic and rheonomic constraints (classical mechanics): Time-independent constraints are scleronomic, while moving constraints are rheonomic. Euler-Lagrange equations remain valid in both cases, but rheonomic constraints modify the structure of the kinetic energy leading to a non-quadratic Lagrangian and generally preventing the identification of the Hamiltonian with "kinetic energy + potential", by opposition to mechanical energy.
    • Hamilton's principle and the action (Maupertuis, Euler, Hamilton): The action is a functional on paths in configuration space. Requiring its first variation to vanish, independently yields the Euler-Lagrange equations and provides a formulation of mechanics that no longer starts from forces or Newton's laws. This is a fully field dependent description.

Electromagnetism
  • Introduction to electromagnetism [Show PDF]

    An overview of the history (experiments and models) that led to modern electromagnetism:

    • Origin and history of the electric charge (~600 BCE - 1897): A brief historical overview of the electric charge: From early electrostatic observations (amber friction) to Coulomb's quantitative law and finally the identification of the electron through Thomson’s measurement.
    • The Millikan experiment and the elementary charge (~1909): A focus on Millikan oil drop experiment: explanation of the setup as well as the theory behind that led to the first evidence of an elementary electric charge. All charges are multiple of this constant. Funnily enough, Millikan experiment has a long controversial history for data falsification.
    • Continuum assumption applied to electromagnetism: charge conservation (~1780–1810): Electromagnetism adopts the same continuum assumption used in fluid and solid mechanics. A charge density field is defined by coarse-graining discrete charges over small volumes, such that integrating the density over a region yields the total charge. This introduces the concepts of charge density, current density, and the charge conservation equation.
    • The first galvanometer: Orsted experiment and Biot-Savart law (~1819-1821): How Orsted observed magnetic loops acting on a compass when a current goes through a wire, and the formalization by Biot and Savart. This allowed to define the first measure instrument for electric currents, galvanometers.
    • Heuristic derivation of Maxwell equations (~1831–1855): An in-depth intuition driven introduction of Maxwell equation. Maxwell-Gauss is a reformulation of Coulomb law with the continuum assumption. Maxwell-Flux is the translation of the conservation of the magnetic flux (Magnetic monopoles have never been observed, so magnetic field lines cannot start or end). Maxwell-Faraday encodes induction (how we nowadays produce electricity), discovered by Faraday, refined by Lenz and reformulated by Maxwell. Finally, Maxwell-Ampère reformulates the Biot-Savart law, with a refinement added by Maxwell linking the magnetic field to the fluctuations of the electric field.

Quantum mechanics
  • From Classical to Quantum Mechanics [Show PDF]

    A list of experiments and theories that progressively led physicists to quantum mechanics:

    • Early atomic models (~1897–1909): First evidence that matter is composed of elementary constituents. Thomson’s discovery of the electron and Rutherford’s nuclear model established the existence of subatomic structure.
    • UV catastrophe & Planck’s quantization (~1900): Crisis in classical electromagnetism caused by blackbody radiation. Planck introduced quantization of energy per radiation mode, introducing the constant that would later be known as Planck’s constant.
    • Bohr–Sommerfeld model (~1913–1916): Semiclassical model for hydrogen-like atoms. Quantization of angular momentum explained spectral lines. Sommerfeld’s extension to elliptical orbits introduced early quantum numbers and partially accounted for fine structure.
    • Photoelectric effect (1887–1905): Observed experimentally by Hertz and theoretically explained by Einstein. The light-quantization hypothesis accounted for the frequency threshold and kinetic energy dependence.
    • Compton effect (~1923): X-ray scattering experiment demonstrating momentum transfer consistent with particle-like photons.
    • Wave–particle duality & de Broglie wavelength (~1924–1927): Extension of quantization to matter. Every particle is associated with a wavelength λ = h/p. Confirmed experimentally by electron diffraction (Davisson–Germer experiment) and later single-particle interference experiments.
